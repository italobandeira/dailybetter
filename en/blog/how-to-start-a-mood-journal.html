Mood journal: what it is and how to start in a few minutes a day

A mood journal is a simple way to note how you feel. Here is how to start without fuss and keep the habit without guilt.

September 30, 2026 · 4 min read · By Italo Bandeira

Do you remember how you felt on Tuesday two weeks ago? Most people don't. Memory holds on to the very good and very bad days, but everything in between fades. A mood journal fixes that with a small gesture: noting, every day or most days, how you are.

What is a mood journal?

It's a regular record of your state of mind. It can live in a notebook, a spreadsheet or an app. The simplest version holds just one thing: how you felt, on a scale with a few options. Fuller versions add a short note, what you did that day, how you slept and your energy level.

The difference from a traditional diary is focus. You don't need to write pages. The goal is a consistent record you can compare over time, so you can look back and see the bigger picture.

Why track how you feel?

  • To spot patterns. After a few weeks it gets easier to notice what repeats: heavier Sundays, better days after a walk, rough weeks when sleep goes wrong.
  • To remember more accurately. Instead of "that month was bad," you can see it was four bad days and plenty of neutral or good ones.
  • To create a pause in your day. Stopping for a few seconds to ask "how am I?" is already a moment of paying attention to yourself.
  • To talk about it more clearly. If you see a health professional, your entries can help you describe how your days have been.

How to start: 5 simple steps

  1. Pick a fixed time. The end of the day works for many people, since you have the whole day to look back on. A reminder helps during the first weeks.
  2. Start with mood only. A five-point scale, from "excellent" to "terrible," is enough. If logging is quick, you'll keep doing it.
  3. Add a sentence when you feel like it. "Lunch with my sister," "tense meeting," "slept badly." One short line gives your future self context.
  4. Don't aim for perfect. Missed a day? That's fine. Log the next one. A journal with gaps still shows far more than no journal at all.
  5. Look back once a week. Take a minute to see your week on the calendar. That's when patterns start to show up.

Common beginner mistakes

Trying to write too much

Long entries are tiring, and the habit fades within days. Go for the minimum entry and save longer writing for when you actually want to.

Only logging bad days

If you only write when something goes wrong, your history gets skewed. Neutral and good days matter as much as hard ones for understanding your rhythm.

Drawing conclusions too early

One week isn't much. Give it at least a month before looking for patterns. And remember that a journal shows correlations, not causes: it helps you ask better questions, not give final answers.

Paper, spreadsheet or app?

Any format works if you use it. Paper is great if you like writing by hand. A spreadsheet gives full control but takes discipline. An app is always in your pocket, reminds you at the time you choose and turns your entries into a calendar and charts automatically.
